# The Black Hills Were Not for Sale

A lot of people already know the Custer story is rotten, and I want to start there because I think that kind of instinct matters. It is not the same as knowing the history, and it should not be mistaken for knowing the history, but it is also not nothing. A person can hear “Custer’s Last Stand” and feel, before they can explain exactly why, that the phrase is doing something gross. The camera is already in the wrong place. The phrase is asking you to look at a white officer’s death and treat the people defending themselves as the scenery around it. The old paintings, the battlefield romance, the “last stand” rhetoric, all of that has the odor of American mythology trying to make its own violence emotionally survivable. So I am not writing this to correct people who already feel that Custer was the asshole. I think that feeling is basically right. I am writing this because feeling that Custer was the asshole is not enough, and can even become a kind of containment if it leaves Custer as the object around which the whole story continues to turn.

That is the annoying thing about Custer, honestly. He is too useful, even as villain. If the story is organized around him, then the available questions stay biographical and tactical: was he reckless, was he ambitious, was he stupid, was Reno drunk, did Benteen fail to move quickly enough, did the scouts warn him, did he underestimate the village, where exactly did he fall, and so on. Some of those questions matter for military history, and I am not saying detail is bad. But there is a way that detail becomes a hiding place, especially when the detail preserves the same dramatic object the old story wanted us to preserve. Even anti-Custer analysis can remain a Custer story. It can let people reject the heroic frame while keeping the officer, the hill, the cavalry, and the battle as the center of gravity. That is not nothing, but it is too easy. It lets the moral satisfaction of hating Custer replace the harder work of understanding the Black Hills land grab as a whole system.

Vine Deloria Jr. knew exactly what he was doing when he titled a book *Custer Died for Your Sins*. The joke lands because it names a real ritual operation. Custer becomes a sacrificial body through which settler America metabolizes guilt without returning anything. His death gives the United States a way to contemplate itself as tragic rather than criminal, wounded rather than obligated, haunted rather than in possession of stolen land. He becomes more useful dead than he was alive, which is saying something, because alive he was already useful to the project of military expansion. But after death he becomes the image through which the United States can keep reprocessing a defeat as a national drama, a masculinity problem, a memory problem, a battlefield question, anything other than a land question. Deloria’s title is not just a joke about Custer. It is a joke about the society that needed Custer to die in a way that could be made spiritually and politically productive for itself.

So I want to move the object. Not away from Greasy Grass because Greasy Grass does not matter, and not away from Custer because he is innocent, but away from the battlefield as the place where the whole story is allowed to begin. The thing to understand is the Black Hills. The thing to understand is how treaty land and sacred relation were converted, step by step, into a mineral resource, then into a fiscal and administrative problem, then into a military problem, then into a ration problem, then into a statutory problem, then into a compensation problem. That sequence matters because the United States did not simply “want land” in some vague way, and it did not simply fail to understand Lakota sacred geography. It ran land through a set of state and market conversions, and each conversion made the previous relation harder to see.

The Black Hills, Ȟe Sápa, Paha Sapa, were not empty country. They were not just beautiful scenery that later acquired symbolic importance. They were not simply one more western mineral district. They were sacred land, treaty land, political land, relational land, and this is where Deloria becomes method rather than decoration. In *God Is Red*, Deloria’s point is not the thin liberal point that Native peoples have “spiritual beliefs” about land and those beliefs should be respectfully considered alongside economic development. That formulation already loses the argument, because it treats sacred relation as an internal cultural value placed on top of an otherwise neutral physical object. Deloria is pushing at something much deeper: place is not a background on which meaning happens. Place is part of meaning. Religious life, political life, memory, ceremony, obligation, and peoplehood are not floating abstractions that can be relocated without loss because they are, in Indigenous worlds, often inseparable from particular mountains, rivers, animals, routes, graves, stories, and responsibilities.

The United States is very bad at hearing that because its own theory of land is already a theory of conversion. Land is made into territory, territory into acreage, acreage into property, property into value, value into compensation, and compensation into the proposed end of the matter. Each step can present itself as practical, legal, modern, or reasonable, but each step also strips something away. By the time the state asks what a fair price would be, it has already smuggled in the assumption that price is the right kind of answer. This is why “the Black Hills were not for sale” is not a slogan sitting on top of the history. It is a theoretical claim about the whole attempted conversion. It says the category is wrong. It says the injury is not only that payment was inadequate, or late, or coerced, but that the proposed substitution of money for land is itself part of the violence.

The treaty has to come before the gold, and not simply as a matter of chronology. It has to come first because otherwise the gold story quietly begins from availability. In 1868, after Red Cloud’s War, the United States entered the Fort Laramie Treaty with the Sioux Nation and Arapaho. The treaty recognized what became known as the Great Sioux Reservation, including the Black Hills, and promised that land for the absolute and undisturbed use and occupation of the people named in the treaty. It also barred unauthorized white settlement or residence in that reservation territory. And there was a cession rule: no part of that reservation could be ceded without the consent of three-fourths of the adult male Indians interested in the land. That detail matters because it makes later American behavior harder to narrate as confusion. The United States had written down the obligation. It had written down the condition for cession. When it could not get that consent, it did not discover ambiguity. It created pressure.

This is one place where American history has to be slowed down, because schoolbook timelines can make the movement from treaty to gold to war feel almost natural. Treaty in 1868, expedition in 1874, battle in 1876, taking in 1877. A sequence of dates can make events look like they followed each other because history was simply moving. But the moral work is in the mechanism between the dates. The treaty did not expire because gold was found. The Black Hills did not become less sacred because miners wanted to enter them. Lakota refusal did not become unreasonable because the United States found the treaty inconvenient. What happened was not the unfolding of frontier inevitability. It was the state learning how to turn its own violation into the premise for further action.

Then comes gold, and gold has to be placed in its actual political economy. If gold is treated only as greed, the story becomes too individual. Of course there was greed. Miners wanted money. Speculators wanted claims. Merchants wanted towns. Newspapers wanted excitement. Railroads wanted routes, freight, and settlement. Politicians wanted development. But gold in the Black Hills enters a United States that is still working through the fiscal aftermath of the Civil War. This is a country of war debt, greenbacks, bondholders, railroad speculation, currency fights, and arguments about what makes federal money credible. The Panic of 1873 had just moved through railroad finance and banking, exposing how much of the country’s future had been borrowed into existence. Jay Cooke & Company collapses because of railroad overextension, banks and markets convulse, and suddenly the question of confidence is not abstract. It is systemic. Then in 1875 the Resumption Act commits the Treasury to redeem legal-tender notes in specie beginning in 1879. Again, I want to be careful here. Black Hills gold did not mechanically cause resumption politics, and I am not interested in pretending one mineral discovery explains the entire postwar fiscal state. But it is equally wrong to treat gold as if it were just a shiny object that made some miners excited. Gold appeared inside a hard-money world where metal, debt, credibility, repayment, and state authority were already entangled.

This is where David Graeber becomes useful, but only if we do not reduce him to “debt is bad.” Debt is not merely an amount owed. Debt organizes moral time. It says something has happened, therefore someone owes, therefore the future is obligated. And quantified debt does something even more specific: it turns relationships into accounts. Once a relation becomes an account, it can be measured, transferred, enforced, settled, audited, refinanced, and closed. In the post-Civil War United States, debt is one of the ways the state relates to its own future. Railroads are not just tracks; they are land grants, bonds, predicted freight, predicted settlement, predicted extraction, predicted repayment. Gold is not just metal; it is confidence, convertibility, proof that paper can be trusted because something supposedly harder stands behind it. Gold in the Black Hills therefore appears as more than possible wealth. It appears as a financeable future.

And once gold enters that world, the Black Hills are not merely invaded by men carrying pans and shovels. They are invaded by a whole grammar of claims. A mine is not only a hole in the ground. It is a title problem, a credit object, a reason for roads, a reason for military protection, a reason for courts, a reason for surveys, a reason for towns, a reason for jurisdiction. This is why the prospector is politically more than an individual trespasser. He is a little jurisdictional crisis moving ahead of the state. He enters illegally, but the illegality becomes productive. His presence creates a problem. The problem produces a demand for protection. Protection produces military presence. Military presence enables roads and settlement. Settlement demands law. Law then retroactively narrates the trespass as the beginning of development.

This is one of the oldest settler moves. Break the line, allow the broken line to thicken into a constituency, and then treat the constituency as reality. By the time the state says it must respond to “facts on the ground,” the facts have already been produced by selective non-enforcement. In the Black Hills, this is not metaphorical. The United States had a treaty obligation to keep unauthorized settlers and miners out. At first the Army was, at least nominally, put in the position of removing trespassers. But as gold interest intensified, that obligation became inconvenient. By late 1875, Grant’s administration had effectively decided that the military would no longer make serious efforts to stop miners from entering the Hills. This is the hinge that I think people need to understand. The United States did not just fail to prevent a problem. It allowed its own citizens to violate treaty land, then treated the presence of those citizens as pressure on the treaty.

That is not confusion. It is governance by induced inevitability. A system produces or tolerates a violation, waits until the violation becomes socially and economically organized, and then says policy must accommodate the reality now before it. It is a very familiar structure if you look for it. A company pollutes, and then cleanup becomes impractical because the local economy depends on the company. Settlers squat, and then removal becomes impractical because families are there now. Roads are built, and then the route becomes inevitable because investment has already occurred. In the Black Hills, miners enter illegally, and then the United States tries to buy the land because the illegal entry has made the treaty inconvenient. The violation becomes the argument for revising the obligation that was supposed to prevent the violation.

The United States tried to purchase the Black Hills in 1875. Lakota leaders refused. I want to keep returning to that refusal because if it is treated as failed bargaining, the whole story gets pulled back into the state’s preferred frame. The refusal was not simply a dispute about price. It was not that the United States had not yet found the correct number. The Hills were not simply unsold. They were not for sale. That difference is the difference between a market problem and a sovereignty problem, between a transaction delayed and a transaction refused. The United States wanted to transform its own breach into an account. It wanted to say, in effect: yes, miners are there, yes, gold has been found, yes, this is now difficult to reverse, so let us find the number that settles it. The refusal said the number is not the issue.

When purchase failed, the state shifted registers. Refusal had to become something else, something the Army could act on. By late 1875, the government ordered Lakota people living away from the agencies to report to the reservations by January 31, 1876, or be treated as hostile. Many of those people were hunting in territory where treaty rights still mattered; the deadline was winter-impossible; the order treated distance, weather, subsistence, kinship, politics, and autonomy as if they could all be flattened into administrative availability. “Hostile” is the crucial word because it is a conversion term. It turns refusal into threat. It turns movement into danger. It turns people who have not made themselves available to the reservation system into military objects. The state no longer has to ask, “Have we honored the treaty?” It can ask, “How do we deal with hostiles?”

That kind of language does violence before the violence. It narrows the field of possible interpretation so force appears as enforcement. A treaty question becomes a security question. A land question becomes a compliance question. A people continuing to live outside the administrative reach of the state become a problem of pacification. This is one of the reasons I get irritated when people talk about “confusion” in American Indian policy as if the basic issue were that everyone misunderstood each other. There were misunderstandings, of course. There are always misunderstandings in cross-cultural, multilingual, coercive, militarized relations. But “confusion” is also one of the ways power excuses itself. The United States did not need to understand Lakota relation to the Black Hills in order to violate it. It only needed to make that relation illegible enough that violation could be routed as policy.

Greasy Grass happens inside this sequence, but it is not the center of the sequence. It is a rupture in the military phase of the conversion process. The Army’s 1876 campaign was not a spontaneous encounter between cultures on an empty frontier. It was a campaign to force non-reservation Lakota and Cheyenne people into submission after gold, trespass, failed purchase, and the conversion of refusal into hostility. Custer and the 7th Cavalry attacked a Native village and lost. That victory matters. It matters because it interrupts the usual story of inevitable conquest. It matters because Native military power defeated a U.S. operation of confinement. It matters because families, horses, lodges, food, ceremony, and political life were defended against attack. But if Greasy Grass becomes the center, especially if Custer remains the center even as the villain, then the larger Black Hills process is pulled back into battlefield memory.

The United States did not become less colonial because it lost a battle. It became more explicit about the coercion it was willing to use. After Greasy Grass, the pressure moved through hunger. This is maybe the most morally clarifying part of the story, because it shows how far the United States was willing to go to make consent appear after consent had been refused. In 1876, Congress attached conditions to Sioux subsistence appropriations: no more rations unless the Sioux relinquished the Black Hills and hunting rights outside the reservation and accepted arrangements the government considered compatible with becoming “self-supporting.” Then a commission brought a prepared agreement, ignored the three-fourths consent requirement in the 1868 treaty, and obtained signatures from only a small fraction of adult Sioux men. Congress then enacted the so-called agreement as the 1877 Act.

That was not a sale. It was hunger being made to perform consent.

And the hunger was not natural. This is important, because otherwise the ration system can appear as if it were merely aid being withheld, rather than dependency being weaponized after the conditions for dependency had been produced. The buffalo economy was being destroyed. Land and mobility were being attacked. People were being forced toward agencies. The United States had helped create the conditions in which rations became necessary, and then used those rations as leverage to force the land transfer that direct purchase had failed to obtain. Rations were not only food. They were an interface of control.

Elizabeth Povinelli’s work on abandonment helps here because she gives us a way to see governance through the management of endurance, not only through spectacular domination. In *Economies of Abandonment*, she writes about how distributions of life and death, hope and harm, endurance and exhaustion are made sensible and just inside liberal governance. The Black Hills case is obviously not the same formation she is writing about, but the mechanism rhymes strongly: a system withdraws or destroys the infrastructures that would let people continue otherwise, then treats survival under imposed dependency as the field where choices can be made. The United States did not simply offer food and then ask for land. It helped produce the need for food, then made food conditional on surrender. That is why “sell or starve” is not rhetorical excess. It is an accurate description of the structure.

This is also where Marx helps, but where Marx alone is not enough. Primitive accumulation is useful because it refuses the comforting idea that capitalism begins in peaceful exchange among free subjects. It begins through enclosure, force, theft, law, dispossession, and the production of people who can be made dependent on markets because their prior means of life have been stripped away. The Black Hills seizure belongs to that history. But if the analysis stops at primitive accumulation, it can accidentally perform another conversion. Sacred land becomes an example of capitalist enclosure, and Indigenous sovereignty becomes one more instance of pre-capitalist relation absorbed by capital. That is true as far as it goes, but it does not go far enough. Deloria has to sit beside Marx because the injury is not only economic incorporation. It is the imposition of a theory of land in which economic value is allowed to overrule sacred relation, treaty obligation, and continuing peoplehood.

Povinelli’s *Geontologies* helps with another part of this, because the Black Hills had to be ontologically redescribed in order to be processed as a resource. They had to become rock, ore, timber, grazing potential, agricultural potential, scenery, public land, federal land, tourist land, mining district. Native relation to the Hills had to be reduced to belief, culture, spirituality, sentiment, claim, or compensable injury. This is one of the quiet operations of extractive power: it does not only take land; it changes the category of what land is allowed to be. Once the Hills are made into mineral-bearing terrain, extraction can appear practical and refusal can appear irrational. Once the land is made into property, compensation can appear to answer theft. Once the sacred relation is redescribed as cultural attachment, the state can imagine itself reasonable for balancing that attachment against development.

The 1877 Act is where law performs its cleaning function. A whole sequence of coercion becomes a date and a citation. Treaty obligation, armed expedition, gold report, illegal mining, non-enforcement, failed purchase, impossible winter ultimatum, military campaign, ration coercion, coerced signatures, congressional abrogation: all of that is compressed into a legal event. This is not to say the legal details do not matter. They matter enormously. But law often stabilizes theft by turning process into form. It gives coercion a document. It gives hunger a statute. It lets later readers say “the Black Hills were ceded” as if the verb itself were innocent.

The Supreme Court, in 1980, recognized some of this. In *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians*, the Court held that the Black Hills taking required just compensation. The opinion recounts the 1868 treaty, the treaty’s protection of the Hills for the undisturbed use and occupation of the Sioux Nation, the three-fourths consent requirement, the gold discovery, the illegal miners, the abandonment of enforcement, the 1876 coercion, the inadequate signatures, and the 1877 Act. In its own legal grammar, the Court could see enough to say that a taking had occurred and that compensation was owed. That matters. I am not dismissing it as nothing. But the remedy was money, and that is the limit of the legal form.

The law can say “taking” and still fail to hear “return.” It can say “compensation” and still preserve the world made by theft. It can calculate principal and interest, but calculation does not transform money into land. It does not transform payment into consent. It does not repair the treaty relation by pricing the violation. This is why the refusal of the money is so important. The compensation has not been accepted. The trust account has grown, and people sometimes describe that refusal as symbolic, impractical, tragic, stubborn, or wasteful. But those descriptions are part of the same colonial grammar because they assume that the money is the resolution and refusal is a failure to accept resolution. The refusal is not a failure to complete the settlement. It is a refusal of the settlement form.

Audra Simpson’s work on refusal clarifies this in a way that ordinary legal language does not. Refusal is not simply saying no. It is not absence from politics or a failure to engage. It is a sovereign political form that interrupts the terms through which the settler state demands legibility. The refused Black Hills money functions this way. It prevents theft from becoming sale after the fact. It prevents compensation from becoming consent. It prevents the account from closing. The United States can say: we took, therefore we owe money. The refusal says: you took, therefore return land. The United States can say: the case has been decided. The refusal says: the occupation continues. The United States can say: the debt can be paid. The refusal says: the relation cannot be priced.

That is continued sovereignty in the present tense. Not sovereignty only as the United States prefers to administer it: recognition, jurisdiction, program management, consultation, federal-tribal procedure, all of which matter but all of which can also become containment. I mean sovereignty as the ongoing capacity of a people to refuse the categories through which another power tries to finish reality. The Black Hills money sitting there is not inert. It is a failed technology of closure. It proves that the United States can occupy, mine, litigate, price, commemorate, and tour the Black Hills, and still not obtain the consent it wants most, which is consent to the conversion of land into account.

This is why the present-tense conflicts matter so much. The Black Hills are not an old grievance sealed inside the nineteenth century. All nine federally recognized Tribal nations in South Dakota have recently supported developing legislation to return federal lands in the Black Hills to the Great Sioux Nation, with the important qualification that this would not involve privately owned lands and would not settle or alter the larger 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty claims. That qualification matters because partial return can become another form of closure if the state treats it as a settlement device. The proposal’s refusal to settle the larger treaty claim is part of the politics. It says land return cannot be converted into another account-clearing instrument.

The extractive pressure also continues. In 2026, tribes and local groups fought exploratory graphite drilling near Pe’Sla, a sacred ceremonial site in the Black Hills, and the company withdrew its plan after legal and public opposition. The mineral changes. The office language changes. The cavalry column becomes a permitting process. The prospector becomes the corporation. The military road becomes the infrastructure corridor. The old phrase “open the Hills” becomes the contemporary language of resource development, environmental review, categorical exclusions, and consultation. I do not mean those things are identical. They are not. But the underlying conversion remains recognizable: land is described as resource, extraction is routed through administrative procedure, Indigenous refusal is treated as a delay or obstacle, and the state’s categories do as much work as the machinery.

This is where Benjamin’s angel of history can be useful, but only if the image is grounded in the material process rather than used as literary seasoning. Benjamin says the storm we call progress drives the angel into the future while wreckage piles up. In the Black Hills, that storm is not abstract. It is railroad finance, gold, debt, military roads, reservation administration, rations, mining claims, federal jurisdiction, monuments, tourism, court judgments, trust accounts, permits, and extraction plans. The wreckage is not simply what was broken in the past. It is the infrastructure that makes the theft appear settled because so much has been built on top of it.

And this is why I think anti-Custer affect, though correct, can satisfy itself too early. A person can reject the heroic cavalry myth, say Greasy Grass instead of Custer’s Last Stand, dislike the paintings, mock Custer, and still not understand the Black Hills as the structure that made the battle possible and the court case inadequate. They can have the right feeling about the wrong story while leaving the land grab under-theorized. That is not a moral failure I am interested in scolding people for. Most of us learned fragments. Most of us were given the battle before the treaty, the officer before the land, the myth before the account. But the anniversary is an opportunity to reorder the facts so the old story becomes harder to inhabit.

The more difficult history is that the United States did not simply tell the wrong story about a battle. It converted sacred and treaty land into resource, resource into claim, claim into debt-backed futurity, debt-backed futurity into jurisdictional pressure, jurisdictional pressure into military classification, military classification into war, war into ration coercion, ration coercion into statutory title, statutory title into property regime, property regime into compensation, and compensation into an attempted closure that Lakota, Dakota, and Nakota refusal continues to interrupt. That is the machine. Custer is one figure inside it, and a useful figure for the machine after his death, but he is not the thing itself.

So when people say the Black Hills are not for sale, that sentence is doing more work than it may seem to be doing. It is not only a demand, though it is a demand. It is not only a moral claim, though it is that too. It is a refusal of commodity logic, a refusal of legal closure, a refusal of the idea that hunger can produce consent, a refusal of the idea that compensation can become return, a refusal of the idea that occupation becomes sovereignty if it lasts long enough and builds enough infrastructure around itself. It says that the United States can calculate damages without receiving consent. It says the treaty did not become morally void because the United States found gold. It says the land is not transformed into money by the court’s remedial imagination. It says the theft is not over because the account has not closed, and the account has not closed because the land has not been returned.

That is what I want people who already distrust the Custer myth to understand. Your instinct is right, but the structure is deeper than the instinct. The story is not just that a bad officer attacked a village and lost, and then the United States told the story from the side of the attacker. The story is that the United States needed the Black Hills to pass through a chain of conversions so that land could become available to capital, to debt, to jurisdiction, to administration, to memory, and finally to compensation. The refusal of payment keeps breaking that chain. It keeps saying the conversion is not complete. It keeps the Black Hills from becoming a settled transaction. It keeps sovereignty present where the United States wants only history.

Greasy Grass should be remembered. The Native victory should be remembered. The fact that the United States Army attacked a village and lost should be remembered. But the battlefield should not be allowed to pull the analysis away from the land. The battle sits inside the Black Hills story, not the other way around. The treaty comes before the expedition. The gold comes before the “hostiles.” The fiscal state comes before the cavalry myth. The hunger comes before the 1877 title. The refusal comes after the judgment and still speaks in the present.

The Black Hills were not for sale. That does not mean the price was wrong. It means the United States has been trying, for a century and a half, to force a form onto a relation that refuses to become that form.
