# The Gray Completion

## How artificial intelligence makes a present that can be left behind

You stop typing, and the future appears in gray.

It sits just after the cursor, short enough not to interrupt you. If you press tab, the gray words become black. They join the sentence as though they had been what you were about to say. If you continue typing, they disappear. Another future will be offered after the next pause.

It is easy to describe this as prediction. The machine looked at the words already written and predicted the words most likely to follow. That description is technically close enough for ordinary purposes, but politically it misses almost everything interesting.

The words in gray are not a forecast of what will happen. They are present now. They have been inserted into the moment when what happens next is still unsettled. They do not tell us the future. They give one possible future a body in the present, where it can be accepted with less effort than it takes to compose another.

This is a small event. Nobody is forced to press tab. Nobody is denied a loan or sent to prison. It is also an unusually clear example of a form of power that is becoming ordinary: the power to represent a situation, generate its plausible successors, and place one successor close enough to action that it can become real.

I have been calling the machinery that performs this operation a **state-realizer**.

That phrase needs to be earned. An AI model is not secretly the government. A token is not a cop. I do not think a transformer has religious beliefs about destiny, or that a line can be drawn directly from Christianity to a software architecture. Those would be colorful ways to avoid the harder argument.

The harder argument begins with Vine Deloria Jr.’s distinction between spatial and temporal religions, moves through the settler state’s need to turn land and relation into administrable states, and arrives at a specific technical problem in probabilistic computation: what must a representation of the present contain before it can be treated as sufficient for deciding what happens next?

The argument is about completion.

Historical destiny promises completion. The administrative state closes cases and completes transitions. A language model completes sequences. These are not the same activity, but they share a political operation: each makes a present that can be left behind.

Place-based obligation offers a different account of completion. An obligation can be discharged without the relation that generated it being exhausted. A treaty is renewed. A seasonal practice returns. A responsibility is met this time, by these people, under these conditions, without becoming finished forever. Time happens because relations continue to make demands.

The difference is not between people who believe in the future and people who believe in the past. It is between two ways of deciding when the present is complete enough to move on.

## The pause is not empty

In 2025, Dhruv Agarwal, Mor Naaman, and Aditya Vashistha published a study of an ordinary GPT-4o autocomplete interface. One hundred and eighteen people in India and the United States completed culturally grounded writing tasks in English, some with AI suggestions and some without them. In the AI condition, a pause in typing requested a short completion from the model. The completion appeared in light gray. Tab accepted it; escape refused it; continued typing ignored it.[^agarwal]

The AI-assisted writing of Indian participants moved toward U.S. norms. A classifier became less able to distinguish between writing by Indian and U.S. participants. The effect was not explained by grammar correction alone. In the researchers’ examples, accounts that named particular ritual practices, family relations, regions, foods, and histories were pulled toward descriptions that were more generic, portable, and familiar to a U.S.-centered model of good English writing. Indian participants accepted more suggestions than U.S. participants and also modified them more often.

The study does not tell us that its participants lost their cultures to a textbox. They chose, refused, and repaired the suggestions. Nor does an experiment involving Indian and U.S. participants stand in for Indigenous political life. It would be a very colonial use of evidence to make one group’s experience available as a metaphor for another.

What the study gives us is a clean view of the mechanism. The model did not wait until the participants had fully stated what they meant and then censor it. It entered during composition, at the point where a partial articulation could still become several different things. Its suggestion was not a command. It was an advantage given to one successor.

The pause matters.

A pause in writing can contain many activities that are invisible to the interface. Someone may be searching for a memory, deciding how much can safely be said, listening for the cadence of the sentence, recalling how an elder described something, noticing that the available language is wrong, or trying to locate themselves within the relations they are writing about. The computer receives none of this as activity. It receives a delay.

The interface treats the delay as an opening for completion.

That is already a theory of time. The unfinished present appears as latency: an interval in which the system could be producing the next thing but is not. Assistance means reducing the interval. Whatever the person might have been doing in the pause has to compete with a future that has already arrived as fluent text.

The model does not have to erase the particular. It can leave every culturally specific noun in place and still change the relation among them. A festival can retain its name while becoming an example of generic celebration. A food can retain its ingredients while becoming an object of universal consumer description. A practice can remain visible as content after losing its authority over the form in which it is being carried forward.

This is what makes generic language politically useful. It is not empty language. It is language whose relations have been made portable.

## Deloria’s religions are theories of authority

In *God Is Red*, Vine Deloria Jr. distinguishes between religious formations oriented primarily through time and those oriented primarily through space. The distinction is often repeated as “religions of time” and “religions of place.” That shorthand can make it sound like Deloria divided the world between Western people who own clocks and Indigenous people who stand still on sacred land.

His argument is more difficult than that. Every religion has time and place. The question is which one authorizes the other.

A temporal religion gives events their meaning through a history that is going somewhere. Creation, fall, covenant, revelation, redemption. The sequence does not merely arrange events. Its promised completion determines what the events are. A place matters because something happened there, will happen there, or must be made to happen there for the story to continue.

Deloria saw the secular descendants of this structure in Manifest Destiny, racial progress, technological development, and the modern state. Christianity did not have to survive intact for its temporal authority to persist. History itself could become the redemptive subject. The promised kingdom could become civilization, development, modernization, communism, green transition, or the properly managed future.

This does not mean these projects are morally equivalent. It means they can share a grammar: the destination authorizes the transformation of the place through which it will be reached.

Under this grammar, land becomes a stage for history. The stage may be cherished, exploited, conserved, restored, or defended. It remains the place where a future conceived elsewhere is supposed to happen.

A spatial religious orientation begins from a different authority. A place is not waiting for an event to give it meaning. Meaning arises through relations among particular beings in particular places. Time does not disappear. It becomes local to the renewal, interruption, maturation, and discharge of those relations.

Rene Ramirez calls the account that can be drawn from Deloria’s work **local time**: time located in place, structured communally, and arranged through cycles rather than detached sequence.[^ramirez] “Cyclical” here does not mean the same thing happens forever. A cycle is not a loop that erases difference. It means an obligation can return because the relation continues. The return is changed by everything that happened during the last passage.

Treaty makes this concrete. In Heidi Kiiwetinepinesiik Stark’s account of Anishinaabe treaty-making, respect, responsibility, and renewal are conditions of treaty relation.[^stark] The written agreement is important, but the relation is not exhausted by the moment of signature. It has to be maintained. A responsibility can be fulfilled without the treaty becoming over.

This gives us two very different meanings of completion.

In one, completion closes the sequence. The unfinished present reaches the state that makes it intelligible and can be superseded.

In the other, completion discharges an obligation. Something has been done, but the relation that made it necessary remains capable of making new demands.

A meal can be finished without kinship being completed. A ceremony can end without the place becoming inert. A treaty obligation can be met without the treaty becoming history. The completion is real. What it does not produce is a world in which the relation has been used up.

That difference will matter when we return to machines that complete things for us.

## How a relation becomes a state

The word *state* carries several meanings that modern governance has learned to keep close together.

To **state** something is to articulate it in a form that can be treated as settled enough to use.

A **state** is the condition a thing is in at a given moment.

The **State** is an apparatus that claims authority to say which conditions exist, which changes count, and which settlements will be enforced.

These meanings are not identical, but they cooperate. The State governs partly by stating the state of things.

It does this through forms that seem too mundane to carry political philosophy: the parcel, the census category, the case file, the permit, the title, the assessment, the recognized tribe, the eligible household, the completed environmental review. Each is an articulation of a situation as a state on which a procedure can operate.

James C. Scott called the production of these forms legibility. A state cannot administer every relation in its full local specificity, so it constructs simplified objects it can see: standardized names, mapped parcels, measured forests, counted populations.[^scott] Ann Laura Stoler’s work on colonial archives makes the complementary point that the archive is not just a warehouse where state knowledge is stored. The archive is itself a practice through which colonial categories, anxieties, and common sense are produced.[^stoler]

I want to make one additional distinction. Simplification becomes temporal governance when the simplified object is treated as **sufficient for transition**.

The operation can be written like this:

**relation → articulation → state → ranked successors → settled successor**

Each arrow changes what can happen next.

A relation is first articulated. Some aspects become names, properties, claims, documents, or data. The articulation is then treated as a state: a representation of how things presently stand. Possible successor states can now be generated and compared. One successor is selected. A procedure, institution, interface, or person settles it as the new state from which the next operation will begin.

None of these transformations has to be total. The state-form does not need to contain the whole world. It only needs enough authority to make everything left out irrelevant to the transition.

That is the crucial move. Relation does not vanish. It is demoted from a condition of action to additional information about an object. Kinship becomes a demographic attribute. Treaty becomes a document in the record. Land becomes a location field. Obligation becomes a preference or value. The parts can all remain represented while losing the power to decide whether the representation is sufficient.

Once a relation has been converted into an attribute, the future can govern it. The question is no longer “what does this relation require here?” It becomes “which available successor best satisfies the objective?”

The objective may be profit, public safety, development, conservation, efficiency, equality, or revolution. Again, these are not equivalent goals. The common structure is that the relation must first appear as a state that can be moved toward a destination.

This is why some radical politics reproduce the time of the state even when they seek to destroy the state that exists. If actual relations are treated as material to be rearranged on behalf of a future condition, then the future remains sovereign. People can be sacrificed to the transition. Places can be treated as strategic terrain. Obligations that do not fit the program become survivals, contradictions, or delays.

The state-form makes this possible because it offers a present that can be declared complete enough to leave behind.

## The Markov question

This brings us to Markov chains.

A Markov chain is not a theory of colonialism. It is a mathematical model of stochastic transition. Its defining property is usually explained by saying that the future is conditionally independent of the past given the present state. Informally: if we know the current state, then the rest of the history gives us no additional information for calculating the next transition.

The important phrase is **given the present state**.

A Markov model does not prove that the past is irrelevant. The modeler defines a state in which the relevant effects of the past are supposed to have been captured. A higher-order process can often be made first-order Markov by enlarging the state until it contains enough history.

The mathematical question is elegant: what representation is sufficient for prediction?

The political question hiding beside it is brutal: who gets to declare the representation sufficient for action?

If a parcel record is the state, relationships that cannot be expressed as properties of the parcel will not constrain the transition. If a case file is the state, histories absent from the file become conditionally irrelevant. If an archive is the state, a living treaty relation may appear only as prior information about a present controlled by someone else.

The problem is not “memorylessness” in the ordinary sense. A state can contain an enormous amount of history. It can contain every available document. It remains a state if that history has been enclosed in a representation that authorizes the next transition.

This is why adding more context does not necessarily restore relation. Relation is not merely history that has not yet been uploaded. It includes authority over what the history can be used to do.

A treaty is not fully represented by a scan of the treaty plus every scholarly work ever written about it if the system using those texts does not recognize the treaty relation as having jurisdiction over the transition. An archive can become infinitely large while the people described in it remain unable to say that a proposed operation does not apply.

State sufficiency is therefore not only an epistemic claim—*we know enough*. It is a jurisdictional claim—*nothing outside this representation has the authority to stop us from proceeding*.

That is the bridge from Markov state to settler state. The bridge is not historical descent. Andrey Markov did not formalize Manifest Destiny. The connection is operational: both need a present whose excluded relations have been made nonbinding for the next transition.

## The archive does not preserve relation

Settler time is often described as erasure, but erasure is only one of its tools. A state can remember a great deal and remain a settler state.

Jean O’Brien’s work on “firsting and lasting” shows how New England histories made settlers the first real inhabitants of a civic present while describing Indigenous peoples as the last members of a disappearing past.[^obrien] The records did not necessarily deny that Indigenous people had existed. They organized Indigenous existence as a completed part of the story.

Johannes Fabian called a related anthropological operation the denial of coevalness. The anthropologist and the people being described meet in the same time, but the writing places the latter in another historical age.[^fabian] The denial is not a failure to know the date. It is a refusal to share authority over the present.

Mark Rifkin extends this problem into what he calls settler time. Indigenous peoples can be recognized as contemporary and still be forced to inhabit a present whose terms are set by settler institutions. “They are still here” is necessary, but it is not temporal sovereignty if the state keeps the power to decide what *here* and *still* mean.[^rifkin]

This helps clarify what an archive can and cannot do. An archive can preserve statements about relation. It can preserve evidence needed to enforce obligation. It can help relations survive attempted destruction. But the archive cannot, by itself, keep a relation alive. If the living parties have no authority over how the record enters the present, preservation can become another form of completion.

The past is not erased. It is made available as context for someone else’s next state.

That sentence describes much of the political economy of artificial intelligence.

## A transformer makes successors, not futures

Large language models are often described as next-token predictors. The description is accurate as long as we do not let the ordinary meaning of “predictor” do too much work.

An autoregressive language model represents the probability of a sequence by multiplying conditional probabilities: each token is generated in relation to the tokens that precede it. A transformer uses attention to construct context-sensitive representations of those preceding tokens. In a decoder model, causal masking prevents the model from looking at later tokens while generating an earlier one.[^vaswani]

The model is not generally a first-order Markov chain over tokens. It can condition on a long prefix, not only on the token immediately before the next one. But the whole retained context can be treated as an expanded state for the generation process. The model receives that state and produces a distribution over successors.

Again, the technical distinction matters. A transformer did not solve the political problem of state sufficiency by remembering more. It made an extraordinary machine for constructing a transition from a very large state.

The context window may contain a conversation, retrieved documents, a system instruction, summaries of previous interactions, tool outputs, user profiles, and examples of desired behavior. It can contain more written history than a person could read during the interaction. It still cannot carry relation merely by carrying text.

“In context” and “in relation” are not synonyms.

A person is in relation when other beings have claims on what they can do, when authority is distributed, when past action creates obligations, when refusal changes what is permissible, when the continuation of the relation depends on how a particular demand is met.

Text is in context when it can affect the model’s probability distribution.

The difference is not mystical. It is institutional. A model may have text saying that a community’s consent is required. Whether that text functions as information or as a binding constraint depends on the systems around the model. Who controls the system prompt? Can the community stop retrieval? Can it inspect retention? Does refusal terminate the workflow? Does the output create a record anyway? Who owns the infrastructure? Which jurisdiction has the power to enforce an answer?

Without those relations, “context” can become a simulation of answerability. The machine can fluently mention obligations that have no power over what it does next.

This is what happened in miniature in the autocomplete study. The model could adapt when enough culturally specific material had already been written. But before that material constrained the continuation, its defaults appeared first. The user had to supply more context or more correction to make the system’s next state fit their relation.

The burden was placed on the person to make themselves sufficiently legible to the completion machine.

## The three realizations of state

We can now return to the state-realizer.

A language model participates in three realizations of state at once.

First, it realizes a **statement**. It turns an unfinished articulation into language that can circulate as though someone had stated it.

Second, it realizes a **computational state**. The prompt, retrieved materials, memory, policies, and prior tokens are assembled into a present sufficient for generating successors.

Third, when joined to an interface and institution, it can help realize a **political state of affairs**. The completion becomes an email, case note, plan, recommendation, form, decision, task, or tool call. Other people and systems encounter it as the new present.

The model does not perform all three operations by itself. This is important. The model generates a distribution. A decoding procedure selects tokens. A product decides which completion to show. A person accepts, edits, or ignores it. An institution decides what accepted text can do. A tool may act on the result.

The state-realizer is this whole arrangement.

Its operation can be written more precisely:

**living relation → represented context → candidate successors → selected completion → operative state**

The political work is distributed across the arrows. Data collection and retrieval govern the first. Model training and inference govern the second. Interface design and ranking govern the third. Human uptake and institutional authority govern the fourth.

Calling the model biased usually collapses this arrangement into one arrow. It asks why the candidate successors are bad. That matters, but it leaves the state-form untouched. A perfectly “unbiased” model would still need the present to be represented as context, still generate successors, and still enter an arrangement that decides which successor becomes real.

The deeper question is whether the living relation had any authority over its conversion into a transitionable state.

This is also why the term *state-realizer* should not be read as a metaphor for government propaganda. The model does not merely repeat the State’s preferred story. It industrializes a form of transition the State has long needed: articulate the present, generate legible next states, settle one, continue from there.

The operation can happen in public administration, but it can also happen in a classroom, workplace, household, mutual-aid project, abolitionist organization, or intimate conversation. Time-governance is not confined to government. It is any arrangement in which control over the transition-ready present becomes control over what futures can be made real.

## Why the frontier is action, not intelligence

Much of the public argument about AI asks when a model will become intelligent enough to act on its own. This directs attention toward the model as an emerging subject and away from the systems that are already making its successors operative.

The important frontier is the shortening distance between completion and consequence.

Autocomplete asks a person to press tab. A chatbot asks them to copy the answer somewhere else. A writing assistant edits the document directly. A retrieval system brings institutional memory into the prompt. An agentic system can call tools, update records, schedule work, send messages, or hand a task to another model. Each step reduces the number of translations between the model’s candidate successor and the next state of the institution.

This is often described as reducing friction. But friction is where a proposed successor can encounter relations omitted from the state.

A person copying text may notice that it sounds wrong. A worker moving information between systems may know that the category does not fit. A meeting may reveal that a “next step” violates an obligation nobody put in the document. Removing those intervals does not only remove wasted labor. It can remove occasions when the state’s claim to sufficiency is challenged.

This does not mean every manual procedure is liberatory. Bureaucracy has always used friction to exhaust people, especially disabled people, poor people, migrants, and anyone whose life does not fit the form. AI can genuinely reduce that violence. Someone can use a model to survive a hostile institution without consenting to the institution’s future.

The question is who controls which friction disappears.

If the person subjected to bureaucracy can automate the institution’s demands while keeping authority over the result, that may redistribute capacity toward them. If the institution automates away the workers, pauses, appeals, and conversations through which its categories could be contested, it consolidates temporal authority.

The same technical capability can participate in opposite political arrangements because the capability is not the relation.

This is why Indigenous protocol cannot be reduced to better data for AI. The *Indigenous Protocol and Artificial Intelligence Position Paper* insists that there is no single Indigenous perspective to install and no substitute for reciprocal relations with particular communities.[^ipai] Protocol is not a cultural attribute attached to the state. It is part of what decides whether the state can be formed and whether a transition is permissible.

Sometimes protocol will shape a system. Sometimes it will place knowledge beyond the system. Sometimes it will require that a completion remain a suggestion. Sometimes it will say that no suggestion should be generated.

The possibility of non-application is part of jurisdiction.

## The radical future has the same problem

It would be comfortable to end by placing the settler state, Christianity, liberal progress, and corporate AI on one side, then placing Indigenous resurgence and abolition on the other. That would give us another ranked set of futures. Choose the good system instead of the bad one.

But a different system is not automatically an alternative if it preserves the operation that made the first system governable.

A radical project can make living relations into states, rank successors according to an emancipatory objective, and settle a future whose authority comes from the program rather than the place. It can demand that obligations wait until after victory. It can call refusal reactionary because refusal interrupts transition. It can treat the people who bear the cost as unfortunate contents of a necessary stage.

The religion of time survives whenever completion is deferred into a future that authorizes the present.

Abolition is often misunderstood in exactly this way: prisons exist now; abolition is the future state without prisons; politics is the transition between the two. Under that framing, abolition becomes a destination, and anything that appears to move us closer can claim some of its authority.

Ruth Wilson Gilmore’s abolition geography is more demanding. Abolition involves changing the material relations that make organized abandonment and premature death ordinary. It is not waiting for the future condition called abolition. It is producing life-giving capacities in the places where carceral relations are being reproduced.[^gilmore]

This makes abolition closer to obligation discharge than historical completion. A person is freed, fed, housed, defended, accompanied. A cage is prevented. A capacity is built. None of these actions completes abolition as a final state. Each changes the relations from which further obligations and possibilities emerge.

Land-based resurgence has a related structure. It is not the projection of an Indigenous future onto neutral territory. It begins from relations whose authority the settler state has tried to make past, cultural, private, or nonexistent. The land is not asked to host the program. The relation to land helps determine what action is.

This is why grounded normativity cannot become an ethics module for a universal AI. Glen Coulthard and Leanne Betasamosake Simpson use the term for ethical frameworks generated through specific Indigenous place-based practices.[^grounded] If the framework is detached from those practices and made portable enough to govern any system anywhere, the relation has once again become content for someone else’s transition.

## Completion without closure

We can now name what a place-based account refuses.

It does not refuse change. It refuses the claim that a representation of the present can become sufficient by stripping excluded relations of their authority.

It does not refuse completion. It distinguishes completing an obligation from closing a relation.

It does not refuse abstraction. Maps, records, stories, models, and categories can all be useful. It refuses the moment when an abstraction is allowed to decide that everything outside it is nonbinding.

It does not refuse the future. It refuses a future that arrives with authority borrowed from the relations it has made into inputs.

This gives us a different way to evaluate AI systems.

The usual questions ask whether the output is accurate, fair, safe, aligned, representative, or useful. We also need to ask what had to be made complete before the model could produce the output.

What became context?

What remained relation?

Who was able to declare the context sufficient?

Which excluded obligations retained the power to interrupt the transition?

What changed when the gray words turned black?

These questions do not produce a universal rule about whether to use AI. They produce political distinctions among arrangements that otherwise look technically similar.

A disabled person using a model to draft an appeal against a bureaucracy is not doing the same thing as the bureaucracy using a model to dispose of appeals. A community-controlled language tool operating under protocols of access and non-circulation is not doing the same thing as a corporation training on whatever text it can acquire. A suggestion that cannot act without renewed consent is not doing the same thing as an agent that updates the record before anyone sees the sentence.

The difference is not located entirely in the model. It is in the relations that can govern the model’s passage from articulation to state, from state to successor, and from successor to settlement.

## The future in gray

Return to the writer at the cursor.

They stop. The system receives the stop as time in which no output is being produced. It states the text as context, generates successor tokens, and realizes one possibility in gray.

The gray matters because it is both real and not yet settled. It occupies the document without fully belonging to it. It is a candidate future made present under a different color.

Then the writer presses tab.

The color changes. The suggestion becomes a statement. The statement becomes part of the model’s next context. What was one candidate successor becomes the state from which later successors will be generated.

This is the smallest possible image of time-governance: a future gains control over the present by becoming the easiest way to complete it.

But the tab key is not destiny.

The writer can stop for a different reason. They can refuse the premise of the prompt. They can ask who needs this sentence and what the sentence will do. They can return to a person rather than a database, to a place rather than a genre, to an obligation rather than an objective. They can use the suggestion against the system that produced it. They can decide the relation is not available for completion.

None of this guarantees liberation. It only keeps the present from becoming sufficient too soon.

That may be one of the practical meanings of a religion of place now: time remains inside obligations because no abstract future, however fluent, is permitted to finish the world on their behalf.
