# What Does It Mean to Believe in Time?

## Vine Deloria Jr. and the politics of where authority lives

We usually use “historical” as a compliment. A historical account is supposed to be deeper than an account of the present because it can explain how the present got here. It can turn the mess of things into a sequence. First this happened, then this, therefore now this.

There is a quiet metaphysics inside that ordinary habit. It assumes that the truth of a place can be found elsewhere: in an origin, a development, a founding, a catastrophe, an inevitable tendency, a promised future. The place is where the story happens. The story is what tells us what the place means.

Vine Deloria Jr. gives us a way to make that habit strange. In *God Is Red*, Deloria distinguishes between religious orientations in which time is primary and those in which space is primary. The familiar shorthand is “religions of time” and “religions of place.” But that shorthand becomes useless if it hardens into personality types: Western people believe in time, Indigenous people believe in place; Christians are linear, Indians are cyclical; modernity moves, tradition stays.

Deloria’s distinction is sharper than that stereotype. His own contrast is between the meaning carried by places and the meaning assigned to historical progression. I want to draw a political question from that contrast that Deloria does not formulate in exactly these terms: where does an order locate the authority that tells it what an event means?

Read this way, in a temporal religious order an event is meaningful because of its position in a larger sequence: creation, fall, covenant, incarnation, salvation. The event happens somewhere, but its authority is not exhausted there. Its position in history lets its meaning travel; place receives meaning from history. In a spatial religious order, by contrast, place is not merely where a universal story happens to have landed. Relations formed in and through particular places have authority, and events matter because they alter those relations. Time does not disappear or collapse into an eternal present. It happens inside obligations belonging to particular communities, lands, kin, more-than-human beings, and treaties; history receives meaning from place.

Everybody knows that things change. The distinction I need for the rest of this essay is jurisdictional: whether change is judged by a sequence outside the relations it rearranges, or whether those relations retain authority over what change can mean. In the argument I develop here, to “believe in time” is therefore not to own a clock or know a calendar. It is to grant a direction of history enough authority to judge a place from outside the relations that constitute it. “Belief in place” is not simply landscape appreciation or a preference for locality. It is the claim that change remains answerable to the relations it rearranges, including the relations through which a future would have to be made.

## Time can become a tribunal

The hardest part of Deloria’s distinction is that it runs against an assumption so basic it is almost invisible: that time is simply the medium in which everything takes place. If time is a neutral container, then a politics organized around progress, development, revolution, transition, or salvation appears merely to have noticed reality. Things are going somewhere. We should decide where we want them to go. The future becomes a blank field of possibility, and politics becomes the work of arranging the present toward it.

But a future can judge the present only after it has been granted authority over the present. A temporal order can turn sequence into a tribunal. The present becomes an unfinished stage answerable to a more complete condition. A people, territory, practice, or relation can then be classified according to where it belongs in the sequence: advanced or backward, developing or stagnant, progressive or reactionary, ready or unready, modern or residual.

Deloria’s argument is specifically concerned with the political consequences of a dominant Christian historical imagination in the United States, but Christianity is not reducible to that form. There are Christianities rooted in particular lands, languages, communities, and struggles, including Christian practices that resist empire rather than authorize it. The point is narrower: when revelation is located in a historical event whose authority is made universal, the relation between God and that event can authorize action far from the place where the event occurred.

Modern historical consciousness did not simply inherit Christian theology unchanged. Reinhart Koselleck’s account of modernity turns on the growing separation between a “space of experience” and a “horizon of expectation.” The future comes to appear qualitatively different from the accumulated past. Walter Benjamin, from another direction, attacked the “homogeneous, empty time” in which every event becomes another point on a smooth line of progress.

What matters here is not whether modern progress is secretly Christian. It is that both religious and secular historical orders can give a larger sequence authority over a particular place. Manifest Destiny, national development, racial civilization, technological mastery, liberal progress, revolutionary transition, and climate modernization are not the same project. But each can be made to say: this place must change because history is going somewhere.

The future can become an alibi for the present transformation of land. This is why claims of inevitability deserve suspicion. A project does not need to be inevitable in order to happen; describing it that way can shift the argument from whether it should happen to how everyone else must adapt. A mine, dam, pipeline, prison, housing development, conservation regime, border, or energy transition can then be presented as a necessary stage in a story so large that the place being changed appears only as an obstacle, resource, or cost. The problem is not having a future. It is allowing a projected future to acquire jurisdiction over the relations that must live through it.

## Settler time makes relation historical

Settler colonialism needs more than a claim to land. It needs ways to make the people whose relations make that land political appear prior to the present that settlers are building.

Jean M. O’Brien calls one version of this process “firsting and lasting.” In New England local histories, settlers became the first inhabitants of a modern civic world while Indigenous people were written as the last members of a world that had already ended. Johannes Fabian described a related operation in anthropology as the denial of coevalness: anthropologist and subject meet in the same time, but the writing places the latter in another time.

Mark Rifkin’s work on settler time clarifies the political consequence. It is not enough to insist that Indigenous peoples are contemporary. Settler institutions can concede contemporary existence while retaining authority to decide what that existence can mean: what counts as tradition, development, governance, evidence, culture, sovereignty, or an actionable claim. The state therefore need not say, “You are in the past.” It can instead say, “Your past may be honored, studied, archived, protected, and commemorated, but it will not determine our present.”

This is why the archive belongs to the politics of time. An archive can preserve what power tried to destroy and can be indispensable to treaty enforcement, land claims, family histories, and political memory. The problem is not that something has been recorded. It is what happens to jurisdiction when a continuing relation becomes a record. A treaty can be perfectly preserved as a historical document while the parties whose obligations it names lose authority over how it governs the present.

Property does something similar in material form. Once land has been constituted as property, a continuing Indigenous relation to it can be acknowledged as prior occupancy, cultural attachment, heritage, evidence, or an earlier claim while the property regime retains authority over what happens now. The relation need not disappear. It can remain visible while being politically downgraded.

Brenna Bhandar and Nicholas Blomley’s recent study of colonial pre-emption in British Columbia makes that transformation concrete. Settlers could acquire proprietary interests through occupation and “improvement” before formal title had fully arrived; law then helped convert those practices into recognized ownership. Their case is British Columbia, not Lakota country. Its relevance here is institutional: a legal sequence can help produce the facts it later treats as settled, and those facts can harden with time.

In *City of Sherrill v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York*, the U.S. Supreme Court rejected the claim that reacquired parcels within the Oneida Nation’s historic reservation thereby resumed sovereign tax status. The Court emphasized nearly two centuries of state and local governance, changed conditions, settled expectations, and the Nation’s long delay in seeking that particular form of relief. The underlying historical relation did not become unknowable. It became less capable of determining present jurisdiction because another order had occupied the interval.

This is more consequential than simple forgetting. A legal order can acknowledge an older claim while letting the duration of the arrangement built over it count in favor of the present arrangement. Earlier decisions become titles, roads, grids, budgets, tax systems, easements, dams, neighborhoods, agencies, and expectations. Those arrangements make some futures cheap and ordinary and others disruptive and exceptional; the accumulated difficulty of changing them can then become a reason not to change them. The past has not vanished. It has been assigned a different political weight.

## A treaty is not just earlier

Recent Oceti Sakowin writing gives us a better way to state the alternative. In a 2026 article for the Ecological Society of America, Phil Two Eagle of the Sicangu Lakota Treaty Council describes responsibility for He SápA through treaties, Creation stories, ceremonies, language, land, water, plants, and other relatives. The force of the claim is not simply chronological priority. “We were here first” still accepts a shared timeline on which earlier and later positions can be ranked. Two Eagle’s account instead concerns continuing relation and responsibility.

A nineteenth-century treaty is old, but its age does not answer whether the relation it names is over. This is the distinction settler chronology repeatedly obscures. A date tells us when a document was signed. It does not by itself tell us whether the obligations constituted through that document have expired, whether the parties remain parties, or whether the relation still has authority over what happens now.

Sarah Hernandez’s *We Are the Stars* makes the same point through Oceti Sakowin literary history. Missionary writing, schooling, translation, print, and settler narratives could displace women’s authority and appropriate land narratives. Yet writing and publication are also among the means through which contemporary Oceti Sakowin writers recover and renew those traditions. The medium does not decide the politics. A record can close a relation, but it can also insist that the relation continues.

Suzanne Kite’s work on Lakota relationships with the stars pushes the argument further. Earth, sky, stars, ceremonial movement, season, and He SápA are spatial and temporal at once. Place does not oppose time. Place can organize time. Ceremonies happen at times; movements follow cycles; generations inherit obligations; stars coordinate where and when together.

That matters because it prevents a cheap reversal of the argument. The alternative to settler historical authority is not timelessness, stasis, or a generic “cyclical Indigenous time.” A place can have a calendar without becoming a stage in somebody else’s history. A placed temporality can be highly organized, future-oriented, and directive. The difference is that the timing remains answerable to the relations through which it has meaning.

## Temporal jurisdiction

This gives us a more precise name for the problem: **temporal jurisdiction**. Jurisdiction usually sounds spatial. Who has authority here? Which court, nation, agency, government, treaty, or legal order can decide what happens on this land? But governing a relation also requires decisions about *when*. When has consultation lasted long enough? When is a claim too late? When has a people demonstrated enough continuity to count as the same political entity? When does a forecast justify intervention? When has an emergency become urgent enough to override ordinary procedure? When has an arrangement lasted long enough to become a settled expectation? When is a treaty historical, and when is it still operative? These are not merely questions about clocks. They determine which relations can act in the present.

Consider pace. A government can recognize a community’s interests and still govern its time. It can invite consultation while deciding when consultation begins, how quickly a claim must be translated into administrable form, and when the window for objection has closed. A transition can be genuinely urgent while the people asked to bear its costs are told there is no longer enough time to renegotiate its terms.

Comparative work with the Tłı̨chǫ First Nation by Angeline Letourneau and Mark Stoddart makes this visible in climate planning. Their point is not that climate urgency is unreal. It is that urgency does not answer the political question of who gets to determine what must happen before a deadline arrives. In that sense, the deadline can perform jurisdiction.

Delay can do the same thing in reverse. An institution can make somebody else wait while the arrangement being challenged continues to accumulate force. Property changes hands. Infrastructure is built. Budgets are committed. Officials retire. Witnesses die. Administrative routines settle. The waiting period is not empty. One party lives inside a continuing order while another waits for the order to decide whether its challenge is actionable.

Continuity is another form of temporal jurisdiction. Federal acknowledgment in the United States makes this visible. For petitioners proceeding under the Part 83 criteria, 25 CFR §§ 83.10–83.11 require substantially continuous identification as an American Indian entity since 1900, community from 1900 to the present, and political influence or authority across that period. The rules expressly say that community and political authority need not be demonstrated at every point in time, require allowance for evidentiary limitations and gaps, and direct the Department to evaluate continuity in historical, regional, cultural, and social context. Even with that flexibility, the administrative process still has to decide which changes, documentary gaps, dispersals, institutional reorganizations, and adaptations count as persistence of the same political entity.

Under these conditions, the archive does not merely report continuity; it helps adjudicate it. The important point is not that all administrative time is colonial, or that deadlines, archives, courts, and evidentiary rules are inherently illegitimate. It is that temporal decisions distribute authority. The institution that decides what counts as too early, too late, continuous, interrupted, settled, urgent, obsolete, or actionable is doing more than measuring time. It is governing which relations can make claims on the present. Temporal jurisdiction names that authority over political timing.

## Territorializing time

Rene Ramirez’s 2025 reconstruction of Deloria gives this mechanism a useful conceptual form. Ramirez calls the pattern he finds across Deloria’s writings “local time.” He is careful that this is his reconstruction, not a finished theory Deloria named and defined. In Ramirez’s account, time is grounded in particular locations, organized through communal relations, and often cyclical in ways specific to the beings and relations involved.

The qualification matters. “Cyclical” does not mean exchanging one universal straight line for one universal Indigenous circle. A plant, person, ceremony, community, watershed, and political relation do not mature according to the same timetable. Their temporal patterns are generated through their particular relations.

Ramirez’s treatment of history is even more useful. History itself is not the problem. Events can be narrated through sequence while remaining rooted in where they occurred and in the relations through which they still matter. What becomes dangerous is abstraction: temporal meaning is removed from the particular place and relations that generated it, then treated as a general measure of what other places must become.

That suggests a more exact account of what I mean by **territorialization of time**. The operation has two movements. First, some aspect of a relation is **delocalized** into a portable temporal form: a date, title, precedent, period, stage, deadline, score, risk category, forecast, or development plan. Then that form is **reterritorialized** inside an institution whose categories and schedules determine what it means and when it acts. A historical relation becomes evidence inside property law; a treaty becomes an old document inside an archive; a forecast becomes an intervention threshold; a period of occupation becomes a basis for title; a long-standing institution becomes a settled expectation; a community’s history becomes an evidentiary chronology another institution judges for continuity.

Portability is not itself the problem. That would make writing, archives, treaty texts, maps, databases, and long-distance solidarity almost impossible to distinguish from colonial capture.

The decisive question is what travels with the representation. Matthew Scobie, Frida Buhre, and Catherine Dussault’s recent comparative work on Indigenous temporal self-determination shows records, storytelling, and research schedules being used under Indigenous authority rather than simply absorbed into settler chronology. Local Contexts’ Traditional Knowledge and Biocultural Labels make a similar point at the level of digital infrastructure: an object can circulate together with community-defined information about provenance, protocol, access, and appropriate reuse. A record is not politically inert because it travels; what matters is whether the authority that governs its interpretation and use can travel with it.

Territorialization of time is therefore not every act of planning, recording, prediction, or historical narration. It is a capture of temporal authority: relations are abstracted into portable forms and then installed inside institutions that can determine what those forms mean, when they become actionable, and what future they authorize. The question, then, is not whether a society has history or a future, but who gets to decide when a relation has become past, what counts as the actionable present, and which future is allowed to command it.

## The future has to answer to somewhere

At this point it would be easy to turn “place” into a softer word for land: modernity treats land as resource, Indigenous people treat land as sacred, therefore politics should learn to value nature. That is not the argument. Place is not an inert object waiting for a better attitude. It is a field of relations in which human and more-than-human beings, practices, memories, capacities, and obligations condition one another. It is political because those relations make claims on what can be done.

Nor can place be reduced to locality. Treaties extend across distance, kinship disperses, watersheds and migration routes cross administrative borders, and records can carry obligations far from where they were created. A place-based politics is therefore not necessarily a politics of staying put. It is a politics in which movement and change remain answerable to the relations that make them possible.

This is also why place does not mean refusing the future. Indigenous futurity scholarship rejects the equation of persistence with stasis. Leanne Betasamosake Simpson’s work on resurgence and Kyle Powys Whyte’s work on collective continuance both describe future-making through the renewal of capacities, practices, ecological relations, and political responsibilities. What is refused is not change but the assumption that colonial dispossession supplies the neutral starting point from which every legitimate future must proceed.

Settler institutions can be comfortable with an Indigenous past because a past can be displayed, taught, commemorated, apologized for, or incorporated into a national history. A continuing Indigenous authority is different: it can make demands on the present and constrain what the state is entitled to call the future. This is the practical difference between recognizing that a relation existed and accepting that it still has jurisdictional force.

Analogous questions appear elsewhere without making those traditions interchangeable. Radical movements, for example, can also allow a promised future to authorize present sacrifice. Ruth Wilson Gilmore’s abolition geography offers a useful contrast because abolition is not simply a redeemed condition that arrives after prisons and policing disappear. It is the production of life-sustaining capacities in places where abandonment and carcerality have been made ordinary. Housing, care, food, education, repair, defense, and collective capacity are part of how a different future becomes possible, not merely benefits postponed until after it arrives.

The political question I have drawn from Deloria’s distinction survives all the qualifications introduced along the way: where does an order locate authority over change? A state can protect a place, memorialize it, consult its inhabitants, or call it sacred while still subordinating its relations to a history whose direction is authorized elsewhere. A politics of place need not refuse planning, movement, technology, or transformation. It asks instead whether those changes remain answerable to the relations they rearrange. The relevant distinction is not between having a future and refusing one, but between a future that arrives as an external tribunal and a future whose legitimacy remains constrained by continuing relations.
